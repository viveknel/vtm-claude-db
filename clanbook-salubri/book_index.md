# Clanbook: Salubri (Dark Ages) — Index

## How to use this file
This is a thematic index. For exact quotes or precise facts, query
book_chunks.db (see README.md in this bundle for query examples) rather
than relying on the summaries below.

## Overview
A sourcebook for *Vampire: The Dark Ages*, framed as a manuscript compiled
by Simon ben-Yaakov, a mortal scrivener entrusted by several Salubri
("Saulot's childer") to record their clan's history and voices before it
is exterminated. The book covers the Salubri's near-total destruction at
the hands of the Tremere ("the Usurpers"), the clan's internal divide
between the **healer** and **warrior** castes (and a disputed, barely-
attested third caste of **"watchers"**), the founder Saulot's ambiguous
nature and mysterious death, the doctrine of Golconda, and game material
(Valeren powers, Merits/Flaws, six character templates). It ends on a
deliberately unsettling historical note that undermines the clan's own
origin myth.

## Chapter One: The Winter's Tale (pp. 7-12)
**Summary:** An opening fiction piece, not analytical text. Lord Benedick,
a Dark Ages nobleman, has sheltered a Salubri healer, "Sister" Genevieve,
in his castle at his mortal wife's — or rather his own — uneasy
discretion, after being warned by unnamed visitors (implicitly Tremere)
that she is a "demon." Benedick lures her to his study with a fake letter
about her "lost kin" so she can be captured for the Tremere. Genevieve
flees, is ambushed by a Tremere warlock threatening Benedick's young son
Hugh, and escapes into the night taking Hugh along "for at least a little
while" to ensure her own safety, promising his safe return. The story
dramatizes the core themes of the book: Tremere persecution operating
through manipulation of mortal hosts, the Salubri's outward gentleness
concealing deep wariness, and the moral ambiguity around who the "monster"
really is.
**Key themes:** Tremere persecution by proxy; hospitality and its
betrayal; the healer's vulnerability despite her power; ambiguous
morality (is taking the child "not murder"?).
**Key terms/entities:** Genevieve (later profiled in Ch.2 and as a Ch.5
character template), Lord Benedick, Hugh, an unnamed Tremere warlock.

## Chapter Two: The Triumvirate (pp. 13-30)
**Summary:** The core lore chapter, structured as compiled first-person
testimonies gathered by Simon ben-Yaakov. Opens with his own framing
letter explaining how he came to be trusted as the Salubri's scrivener,
dedicating the work to "Trisala, grandchilde of Saulot." Establishes that
the Salubri divide into two clear castes — **healers** and **warriors**
— plus a barely-attested, distrusted third caste of **"watchers."** The
clan is deeply solitary and has scattered since the Tremere persecution
began, both by nature and for mutual safety (captured Salubri can't
betray clanmates whose location they don't know).
- **Way of the Warrior:** Traces the warrior caste to Samiel, Saulot's
  restive childe, trained by Brujah and Gangrel because he chafed at the
  healer's placid life. Recounts the Baali Wars (Samiel leads a mixed-clan
  force into the "valley of Gehenna"/vale of Gehenna after Baali diablerize
  two Salubri warriors), Samiel's death (assassinated in battle by a
  traitor within Salubri ranks — see "The Infernal" below), and how five
  surviving grandsires (Uriel, Za'aphiel, Ezrael, Aariel, Gabriel) rebuilt
  the warrior line. Describes warrior selection (skill of arms, hard
  conviction, restive spirit, direct calling), training (7-year process
  for the narrator Kadiel, culminating in a solitary vigil and a formal
  "blooding" ritual distinct from the Embrace), the largely-lost **Code of
  Samiel** (believed dictated by the archangel Michael), and warriors'
  strict but not absolute code (no killing for sport, no boasting, no
  gratuitous savagery — "we do what must be done, but take no joy in
  slaughter"). Notes female warriors are accepted but some clans/orders
  refuse to train them. Covers "Fallen Angels": warriors who renamed
  themselves after fallen angels and left in blasphemous rage after
  Saulot's diablerie, raising an unresolved question of whether the
  clan must someday hunt its own.
- **The Song of the Healer:** Saulot himself sought "greater healing" and
  created Valeren from his own blood plus teachings of Eastern elders;
  emphasizes meditation/purity practices as prerequisite to keeping the
  gifts from "taint." Recounts Saulot's own biography (favored by Caine,
  close to his brother Malkav, journeys east — likely India then Cathay
  — discovery of Golconda after meeting a sage, later corrupted/troubled
  visions, and final torpor after a second departure east). Covers the
  Embrace and training customs of healers (slow, careful selection by
  character-testing; in the "old days" long apprenticeships, now hasty
  due to the hunt), profiles of notable healers (Genevieve du Fortin;
  Rayzeel, said to be one of Saulot's favorites and possibly his lover;
  Matthias, partnered with the warrior Yael against the infernal).
- **The Boke of the Watcher:** Deliberately uncertain, second-hand
  material about a possible third caste. Includes a first-person account
  from "Pazia," who frames healers and warriors as "sisters" and
  "brothers" respectively and whose tone toward warriors strikes the
  narrator as oddly intense, plus an intercepted letter to "Master Simon"
  from a self-described "watcher" describing covert surveillance of
  Ceoris (the Tremere stronghold) and claiming the watchers know "a
  strange and mighty secret" about how Tremere diablerized Saulot —
  ending with a call to arms for "brothers" to ready for battle. The
  chapter's editor (ben-Yaakov) notes the letter conspicuously omits any
  mention of healers, leaving him with "unpleasant conclusions."
**Key themes:** Two/three-caste structure; the Baali Wars and betrayal
from within; Golconda's origin in Saulot's own journeys; the still-open
question of whether "watchers" are a genuine caste, Tremere spies, or a
faction manipulating the warriors.
**Key terms/entities:** Samiel, Saulot, Caine, Malkav, Uriel/Za'aphiel/
Ezrael/Aariel/Gabriel (warrior grandsires), Nuriel, Kadiel, Scatha-
Columbkille, Yael, Matthias, Rayzeel, Genevieve, Ceoris, the Code of
Samiel, Golconda, Valeren.
**Notable claims/data points:** Scatha-Columbkille's Embrace dated to
~700 AD; Samiel had 15 warriors at Gehenna; only 5 warriors survived to
found the modern warrior lineages; Genevieve was ~10 years into unlife
when she met the narrator.

## Chapter Three: Miscellanea et Demonica (pp. 31-42)
**Summary:** A mixed reference chapter.
- **Regarding the Third Eye:** Presents competing, unresolved theories —
  most support a link to advanced Valeren use, but its ultimate purpose
  (per legend, seeing into "the spirit world" or "the naked souls of men")
  is believed lost even to the clan itself.
- **Relations with Others:** The chapter's signature device — paired
  contrasting quotes from a warrior (Scatha-Columbkille) and a healer
  (Gennadios) on every other major clan/faction: Assamites (grudging
  mutual respect after Crusade-era battles), Brujah (old friendship
  fraying as Salubri become "legendary" rather than known first-hand),
  Cappadocians (useful but unsettling), Followers of Set (predatory
  "reform" scams), Gangrel ("rough friendship," but Gangrel now see
  Salubri as weak for tolerating the hunt), Lasombra (untrustworthy,
  opportunistic), Malkavians (deep historic bond via Saulot/Malkav,
  healers still repay old debts), Nosferatu ("the Lepers'" sympathy has
  materially protected hunted Salubri), Ravnos (unpredictable but
  occasionally loyal), Toreador (frustrating indifference), **Tremere**
  (uniform, uncompromising hatred — "the Usurpers"), Tzimisce (a genuinely
  useful alliance via hospitality customs and shared Tremere-hatred, with
  persistent unconfirmed rumors of something deeper), Ventrue (mixed:
  some shelter Salubri out of real honor, others cave to Tremere
  pressure), Baali (kill on sight, no exceptions), Werewolves (wary but
  occasionally warm — see Shepherd's Watch), Magi/theurgists (deep
  distrust, "they spawned the Tremere"), Wraiths (feared/pitied), Fae
  (the Fae avoid Salubri, to the clan's regret).
- **Relations with Mortals / On Ghouls:** Salubri cultivate ordinary
  mortal community ties for genuine safety (neighbors who'll raise a
  "hue and cry" against a spying ghoul); the clan apparently does not
  create ghouls at all — an all-or-nothing view of the vampiric state
  makes the ghoul's halfway status "self-inflicted damnation."
- **On Loneliness:** An impassioned essay arguing the hunted Salubri's
  isolation is a harsher, involuntary version of any other clan's chosen
  solitude.
- **On Feeding:** Saulot forbade healers from feeding on anyone who fears
  them, forcing healers to cultivate trust with mortals; warriors take a
  more "legalistic" view and feel free to feed on hated enemies in battle
  — a further healer/warrior philosophical rift.
- **On the Kiss:** Two near-absolute Salubri commandments on the Embrace:
  never Embrace a child, and never Embrace out of love/for companionship
  (reasoned as protecting the childe from resenting the sire for
  inflicting "horror" on them) — immediately complicated by the persistent
  rumor that Saulot himself broke this rule with Rayzeel.
- **On Saulot:** Surveys wildly conflicting physical descriptions and
  moral characterizations of Saulot (from "near-deity" to "near-inhuman"),
  concluding the truth lies between the extremes.
- **The Infernal:** A separate co-authored fragment (by the warrior Yael
  and the healer Matthias, complete with a partial genealogy back to
  Caine) recounting the Baali Wars in more detail: Saulot sent four
  warriors to scout Baali activity in "the river valleys"; only one
  returned, driven half-mad, reporting two Salubri had been diablerized;
  the ensuing war killed Samiel himself, betrayed by a still-unidentified
  traitor from within the Salubri ranks (called "Ahab" by later
  storytellers, detailed further in Appendix Two). Also covers the later,
  ethically fraught **Gnostic Purges**, in which Saulot hunted a
  Gnostic-adjacent Christian sect believing the traitor sheltered among
  them, and firmly denies the existence of any "Via Dolorosa" attributed
  to the Salubri as Tremere-originated slander.
**Key themes:** The clan's total inversion of status ("once pearls...now
hunted"); the healer/warrior philosophical rift recurring across feeding,
the Kiss, and views of other clans; persistent doubt about Saulot's true
nature; the unresolved traitor mystery.
**Key terms/entities:** the Baali Wars, the Gnostic Purges, "the Via
Dolorosa" (disputed/denied), Yael, Matthias, "the Traitor"/Ahab.

## Chapter Four: The Powers of the Righteous (pp. 43-48)
**Summary:** Game-mechanical chapter (Vampire: The Dark Ages rules).
Covers **Powers of the Blooding** — abilities available only to warriors
blooded via an authentic Code-of-Samiel ritual: *Tracker's Mark* (reading
information from a target's blood) and *Blessing of the Name* (a
Presence/fear-inducing, wound-ignoring combat state invoked by calling on
the warrior's namesake angel, usable once per story, with in-fiction
warnings against invoking it for unworthy reasons). Then **New Valeren
Uses** at various levels: Auguring the Sickness (••, diagnostic),
Peacemaker (•••, area calming aura, healer/watcher power), King David's
Blessing (••••, music-based healing/anti-Derangement aid, created by
Rayzeel to comfort Saulot — "Rayzeel's Song"), Sword of the Righteous
(•••••••, flaming weapon vs. Baali/shadow powers), Watching the Passage
(••••••, draws knowledge/healing from a recently deceased "Ending the
Watch" recipient), and Heaven's Gate (••••••••••, the rarest — can revive
a very recently dead body without Embrace or ghouling). Closes with
Merits and Flaws specific to the clan: Scent of the Other, Protected,
Warrens, Sight Beyond Sight, Blooding by the Code (Merits); Odd Eye,
Stubborn, Unblooded, Visible Eye (Flaws).
**Key themes:** Mechanical reinforcement of the healer/warrior divide
(most high-level Valeren powers are healer/watcher-flavored except the
warrior-only Blooding powers and Sword of the Righteous); Golconda-
adjacent restraint built into how the most powerful abilities work.

## Chapter Five: The Hosts of Heaven (pp. 49-62)
**Summary:** Six ready-to-play character templates, each with a quote,
prose backstory, concept line, roleplaying hints, and starting gear —
paired with character sheets. **The Holy Thief** (a reformed thief-turned-
relic-recovery agent who steals sacred/Salubri items back from corrupt
clergy and Usurpers alike); **Electra** (a vengeance-driven ancient
warrior modeled on the mythic avenger, sworn to destroy every Tremere
regardless of cost, lairs with a Malkavian companion); **Searcher into
God's Mysteries** (a healer obsessively studying anatomy/astronomy/
Greek-Arabic texts, sheltering uneasily with the Tzimisce in Carpathia);
**Demon Hunter** (traumatized after a year-long ordeal in a Baali lair
as a blooding trial, now a grimly devout Baali-hunter newly enraged by
Saulot's diablerie); **Court Advisor** (a scribe-turned-vampire who
carved out real political security in a Ventrue-led court and works
quietly against Tremere influence there); **The Battlefield Healer**
(a self-taught field medic Embraced after impressing a Salubri healer
with unschooled surgical skill, now surviving by serving a Brujah
warband).
**Key themes:** Each template embodies a different survival strategy
under the hunt (theft/subterfuge, vengeance, scholarship, holy war,
political embedding, mercenary usefulness) — collectively illustrating
"the Days to Come" theme from Chapter Two.

## Appendix One: The Dwindling Few (pp. 63-66)
**Summary:** Short in-character profiles of a handful of named Salubri
who may (per the narrator, with real uncertainty) still walk the earth.
**Rayzeel** — presumed one of Saulot's favorite childer and possible
lover, a healer/musician whose surviving song fragments are treasured;
vanished after Saulot's torpor, fate unknown (deified or dead), with a
postscript noting the narrator recently heard buskers in Salerno singing
something resembling her style. **Yael and Matthias** — the warrior/
healer demon-hunting pair introduced earlier, described as inseparable;
recent news has left the narrator fearful for them. **Nuriel** — widely
regarded as the greatest living warrior and a candidate to become a new
Samiel-like leader, though he personally dismisses the idea. **Jack-o-
Daws** — an English rogue who steals Salubri manuscripts/relics back
from those (including Tremere Magisters) who've seized them; believed to
avoid his own sire out of misplaced shame rather than estrangement.
**Key themes:** The fragility and uncertainty of even the clan's most
notable survivors; hope threaded through loss.

## Appendix Two: A Historical Note, of Interest to Scholars and Slanderers (pp. 66-69)
**Summary:** Simon ben-Yaakov's unsettling closing note. First profiles
"Ahab" — the still-unnamed-until-now traitor blamed for Samiel's death
and suspected of continuing infernal work, possibly complicit in
Tremere's diablerie of Saulot. Then the narrator describes receiving an
apparently ancient Sumerian-style stone tablet of uncertain provenance
that recounts, in consistent, widely-corroborated (if third/second-hand)
form, the Baali origin myth: the last three survivors of a destroyed
demon-worshipping cult, thrown into a well, were each given just enough
vitae by a mysterious Cainite stranger to survive and fester into what
became the Baali. Every prior telling the narrator has collected refused
to name the stranger. This tablet does: **it names him Saulot.** The
narrator resolves to destroy the tablet and this record of it — and
closes with the line "Would that I were so lucky," leaving the claim
deliberately unresolved and deeply destabilizing to everything the book
has built about the clan's righteousness.
**Key themes:** This is the book's thesis-complicating final twist —
directly undercutting Chapter Two/Three's more sympathetic portrait of
Saulot and reframing the entire Salubri self-narrative as possibly
resting on a founder who (however unknowingly) created their sworn
enemy. Deliberately left as unresolved, disputed testimony rather than
confirmed fact.
**Key terms/entities:** Ahab (the Traitor), the Sumerian tablet, the
origin of the Baali.

## Cross-cutting themes
- **The healer/warrior rift** recurs in nearly every chapter — different
  views on feeding, the Kiss, dealing with other clans, and which Valeren
  powers each caste tends toward — and the maybe-real "watcher" caste
  sits suspiciously outside/behind that divide.
- **Persecution and its costs**: nearly every named character (Genevieve,
  Yael/Matthias, Nuriel, Jack-o-Daws, the templates in Ch.5) is defined by
  a personal strategy for surviving the Tremere hunt.
- **Saulot's ambiguity**: built up gradually from "confusing, contested
  legend" (Ch.3) to the explicit implication in Appendix Two that Saulot
  himself may have created the Baali — the single biggest unresolved
  question in the book.
- **Golconda** as the clan's shared spiritual aspiration, achieved more
  easily by healers than warriors by design of their respective
  disciplines.
- **Nested/unreliable narration**: virtually everything is reported
  speech (ben-Yaakov transcribing others, Yael/Matthias's own compiled
  fragment, Pazia's testimony, the tablet of uncertain provenance),
  consistent with the book's repeated point that Salubri history survives
  only as contested oral tradition.
