# Dark Ages: Inquisitor Companion — Index

## How to use this file
This is a thematic index. For exact quotes or precise facts, query
book_chunks.db (see README.md in this bundle for query examples) rather
than relying on the summaries below.

**This book is a direct companion to `dark-ages-inquisitor`, not a
standalone title.** It assumes the base book's premise, cosmology, and
core mechanics (Superior Virtues, Conviction, Piety, Callousness,
Curses) and expands specifically on the five orders' internal life,
recruitment, chapter-houses, and a large catalog of new Blessings,
Curses, and Merits/Flaws. Named characters from the base book —
Cardinal Battista Marzone, Gauthier de Dampiere, Aignen le Libraire,
Rodrigue de Navarre, Osoro of Madrid, Leopold von Murnau — recur here
as established figures rather than being reintroduced. If a question
concerns the shadow Inquisition's founding, the Crimson Curia/Cainite
Heresy plot, or core mechanics, check `dark-ages-inquisitor/book_index.md`
first; this file covers what that one doesn't.

## Overview
*Dark Ages: Inquisitor Companion* (White Wolf, 2004) is the first and
only dedicated sourcebook for *Dark Ages: Inquisitor* (2002), describing
itself as a "catechism" — filling in procedural and cultural detail the
core book didn't have room for, rather than introducing new premise. Its
four authors split the book by section (credits, p.2): Kraig Blackwelder
(The Ways of the Faithful), Myranda Kalis (Life in the Church, the
Inquisition in the Church, Soldiers of God/Servants of Hell), Jonathan
L. Shepherd (Sample of Play, Orisons and Holy Art), Adam Tinworth (My
Order, My Brothers), and Janet Trautvetter (Prelude, Serving the
Inquisition). It covers, in order: expanded recruitment/tactics/politics
for each of the five orders plus a sample chapter-house apiece (Ch. One);
the medieval Church's own monastic structure and how the Inquisition's
Marzonian Rule governs mixed-order, mixed-gender cells within it, plus a
fully worked Sample of Play (Ch. Two); the subjective experience of
Conviction, Blessings, and Curses, a full new Holy Art path (Corpus Lux)
and eight new *ritae*, and per-order catalogs of new Curses and
Merits/Flaws (Ch. Three); and cell formation/politics, rules for
un-empowered inquisitor characters recruited from outside the five
orders, guidance on inquisitor interaction with vampires/werewolves/
mages/fae, and six new antagonists (Ch. Four).

## Prelude: Woman's Intuition (pp. 5-13)
**Summary:** A fiction piece introducing **Sister Mathilde von Murnau**,
a Red Sister posted to Lübeck, niece of Brother Leopold von Murnau (the
base book's protagonist) and a member of the House of Murnau who
explicitly does *not* share her uncle's detection gift — her own
Blessing is subtler, "a good instinct about people." Alongside **Sir
Baudioun le Breton** (Poor Knight), **Sister Katherin**, **One-Eyed
Huart**, and **Brother Jander**, she investigates a woman's (Agnise's)
coerced confession implicating a merchant, **Master Nicolaus von Mainz**,
in demon-summoning. Mathilde notices the confession's internal
inconsistencies (Nicolaus can't stand the smell of lamb, yet the
confession has him serving it; a date that doesn't match his known
travel) and deduces Agnise was fed a name to give under duress. Trailing
a suspicious "brother" (Martin) leads the cell to a barge where Mathilde
is nearly caught by a young, aristocratic-seeming Cainite and his ghoul
**Grim** — she escapes exposure only by inducing her own (canonical
Murnau-curse) nausea as a distraction, and Sir Baudioun and Sir Augustin
arrive to stake the vampire and destroy Grim. The story closes with the
cell moving to arrest the real culprit, **Master Baldewin** of the Salt
Merchants' Guild.
**Key themes:** Interrogation methodology and its failure modes (a
confession shaped by leading questions rather than truth); the Murnau
curse as involuntary and humiliating rather than heroic in the moment
(vomiting as an emergency distraction, not a power); women running
field investigations under cover of "unconspicuous" disguise.
**Key terms/entities:** Sister Mathilde von Murnau, Sir Baudioun le
Breton, Sir Augustin, Sister Katherin, One-Eyed Huart, Brother Jander,
Agnise, Ilse, Master Nicolaus von Mainz, Martin, Grim, Master Baldewin.

## Introduction (pp. 15-18)
**Summary:** Frames the book as a "catechism" of procedural detail
rather than new premise, previews each chapter, and closes with a
**Vocabularium** of period monastic/Church terms used throughout the
book (Abbot/Abbess, Advocate, Anchorite/Anchoress, Armarium, Beguinage,
Clerg, Cloistering, Dowry, Hospice, Leprosaria, Novice, Oblation, Opus
Dei, Preceptory, Rector, Regular Clergy, Secular Clergy).
**Key terms/entities:** Rector (a cell's designated leader — distinct
usage from a monastery's own "rector"/prior), the Daggers of God
(previewed here, detailed at p.82).

## Chapter One: My Order, My Brothers (pp. 19-50)
**Summary:** Order-by-order deep dive: recruitment methods, the order's
characteristic approach to identifying/fighting "the Enemy," its
relationships with each of the other four orders, its relationship to
"the Flock" (the general laity), and one fully detailed sample
chapter-house. Each order gets its own epigraph-quote and in-fiction
recruitment vignette.
- **The Poor Knights of the Passion of the Cross of Acre** (pp. 20-25):
  recruits mainly from other military orders' "forsworn" knights and
  from inquisitors' own sons (trained at dedicated academies attached to
  each of the order's eight "tongues"), plus direct postulants tested on
  arms and theology. Classifies enemies by combat behavior rather than
  taxonomy — **demon animals**, **demon-tainted men**, **warlocks**, and
  **true demons** each get a named three-pillar tactical doctrine
  (surprise/numbers/skill; observation/planning/ambush; observation/
  stealth/speed; ruthlessness/caution/faith, respectively) — and admits
  it has no real strategy against ghosts/fae/spirits. Distrusts the Red
  Order's magic-adjacent Holy Art and is uneasy with Red *Sisters*
  specifically; respects the Sisters of St. John and the House of
  Murnau; resents taking direction from the "common" Oculi Dei.
  Chapter-house: **the Hospice of St. James the Elder**, Compostela
  (p.25) — run by seneschal Laurgain (a skin-changer attack survivor),
  priest Berin of Lombardy, and Commander Hunerct; its live plot hook
  involves a demon cabal targeting its pilgrim trade, secretly tracked
  by an Oculi Dei cell under Rodrigue de Navarre.
- **The Red Order of St. Theodosius** (pp. 26-32): the most
  intellectually selective order — postulants undergo a month of
  theological "temptation" interrogation by senior Theodosians before
  only three per region per season are accepted; a secondary "Apprentice"
  track recruits gifted children, some suspected (never confirmed) to be
  Oculi Dei offspring. Builds a working **Hierarchy of Demons** (Diabolic
  → Malefic → Vampiric → Animalistic → Spiritual → Corrupted, pp.29-30)
  from direct encounters, folklore, and captured documents — vampires are
  explicitly classified by three weaknesses (sunlight, faith, fire) and
  are held to have no soul left to redeem. The order's stigmata (see
  sidebar, p.30) is its main credibility-builder with the Supreme
  Council, complicated by "other" (non-Biblical, disease-like) stigmata
  some Theodosians privately fear signals deviation. Chapter-house: **the
  Abbey of St. Denis**, Paris (pp.31-32) — Abbot Gervèse le Fèvre, Doctor
  Caschin d'Anjou, researcher Mathieu of Gascony, Commander Renouart de
  Marseilles; hunting sewer-dwelling vampires while eyeing the newly
  under-construction Notre Dame as a potential chapter-house, a project
  complicated by a group of secretive alchemists with unclear (possibly
  Hermetic mage) affiliations already using it.
- **The Sisters of St. John** (pp. 32-37): recruits both women already
  manifesting the "Holy Sight" (about 1 in 5 novices; nearly 2 in 5 show
  exorcism aptitude instead) and ordinary devout women, often via
  transfer from other convents that find prophetic novices disruptive.
  Deliberately **simplifies and reinterprets** individual Sisters'
  visions before relaying them to other orders — a policy Mother
  Maria-Immaculada defends as necessary but that breeds mistrust.
  Respected by the Knights of Acre and the House of Murnau; uneasy about
  the Red Order's academic coldness (a deliberate cross-posting
  experiment to soften the Red Sisters specifically has failed); deeply
  afraid for the Oculi Dei's isolation, and has petitioned to let
  departing Sisters transfer into that order. Chapter-house: **the
  Convent of St. Cecilia**, Amberley, Sussex (pp.37-38) — Mother Superior
  Laetitia, Sister Agnes of Kent (whose visions come from the restless
  dead), exorcist Sister Fryswyde, and Winifred (secretly an Oculi Dei
  liaison); currently facing a suspected shape-changing-witch threat the
  convent is reluctant to summon the Knights of Acre against.
- **The House of Murnau** (pp. 38-44): the only lay/noble order — not so
  much recruited as gradually informed, since the family curse itself is
  hereditary. Head of family **Frederick von Murnau** authorized the
  whole House's induction into the Inquisition; his twin sister
  **Franziska** vets all marriages into the family for hidden
  corruption. Classifies its own targets informally by *personal
  attitude* rather than nature — **Politicians** (demons in worldly
  power, the House's specialty, handled through social/financial
  undermining before confrontation), **Predators** (left mostly to
  younger Murnau blooding themselves, and to the Knights), and
  **Corruptors** (temptation-demons, handled only on direct order from
  Frederick/Franziska, with total destruction of the demon's remains
  afterward rather than the House's usual asset-seizure). Views the
  Knights as useful brute force, respects the Red Order's scholarship
  more than it admits, and treats the Oculi Dei as useful social
  inferiors. Chapter-house: **the Munich residence of Heidolfus von
  Murnau** (pp.42-44) — Heidolfus, half-sister Aveline, Father Munifrid,
  and Hugo (a farmer-turned-strongman); secretly hunting the "hoi-hoi
  man," a local folklore figure who is actually the demon **Hoilial**
  wearing the body of Heidolfus and Aveline's vanished father, Bodoloff.
- **The Oculi Dei** (pp. 44-49): the only order with no formal
  application process — candidates are quietly tested for months before
  even being approached, drawn from three pools: **the Skilled**
  (deliberately recruited for discretion, faith, and native intelligence
  over education), **the Witnesses** (survivors of supernatural
  encounters), and **the Unlucky** (people who stumbled onto the order's
  existence and are given the choice to join or die). Mobile Eyes are
  promoted from among stationary cells through an anonymized, code-named
  nomination process. Their intelligence work is organized into four
  categories — **habits**, **fellowship**, **sins**, and **mysteries**
  — the last of which has already let them positively identify vampires
  as blood-drinking, daylight-avoiding creatures, though not (yet)
  werewolves by any reliable pattern. Sidebar (p.45): **the Sword of St.
  James**, the Oculi Dei's own secret military progenitor order, now
  split into two rival factions — Rodrigue de Navarre's paranoid
  loyalists (hunting supposed traitors) versus Aignen le Libraire's
  reformed Swords (protecting Eyes in the field); this split is unknown
  to the wider Inquisition and to most Eyes themselves. Sidebar (p.46):
  **Thomas' Men**, an allied English/French spy network descended from
  St. Thomas Becket's exile-era intelligence operation, now merged with
  Aignen's faction and covertly watching Rodrigue. Chapter-house/cover:
  **Everard's Players** (pp.49), a traveling theater troupe touring
  northern France whose leader Everard d'Arras is unaware his star
  actors Julien and Varocher de Saumur are mobile Eyes fleeing
  Nosferatu who have identified and are stalking them.
**Key themes:** Institutional distrust as a structural feature of the
shadow Inquisition, not incidental friction — every order's relations
section names specific grievances with specific others; each order's
Enemy-classification scheme reveals what that order is actually built
to fight (Knights: combat behavior; Red Order: taxonomic theology;
Sisters: individual suffering/redemption; Murnau: personal motive/
temperament; Oculi Dei: observable pattern).
**Key terms/entities:** Sir Ollier, Fridebraht von Graft (the "Errant
Postulant" who rejected the Red Order and later reappears with
unexplained Holy-Art-like powers, p.27 — an unresolved plot hook), Brother
Giordano Nicola d'Arzenta (Red Order's Byleth expert — see p.138),
Frederick von Murnau, Franziska von Murnau, Otto von Murnau, Leopold von
Murnau, Rodrigue de Navarre, Aignen le Libraire, Simpkin Cotter.

## Chapter Two: Playing the Inquisition (pp. 51-89)
**Summary:** Two halves: real-world grounding in medieval Church
structure (so Storytellers can portray it credibly), then the
Inquisition-specific social rules that govern mixed cells.
- **Life in the Church** (pp. 51-58): history of Christian monasticism
  (eremitic vs. cenobitic monasticism, the Benedictine Rule, p.53
  sidebar), the canonical-hours daily schedule (Nocturns through
  Compline), monastic offices (abbot/abbess, prior/prioress, cellarer,
  sacrist, almoner, precentor, infirmarian, etc., pp.55-56), monastery
  land rights/patronage under canon law, and **corruption within the
  Church** (p.57) as a real, distinct problem from supernatural
  infiltration — simple vow-breaking handled in-house versus the more
  corrosive cultivation of wealth/political influence and doctrinal
  ignorance among poorly-educated clergy.
- **The Inquisition in the Church** (pp. 58-79): explains **secular
  ordination** — the Minor Orders (Porter, Reader, Exorcist, Acolyte)
  and Major Orders (Subdeacon, Deacon, Priest) — as the path available
  to male inquisitors who don't want full monastic vows. Gives each
  order's own daily schedule and command structure in turn (the Poor
  Knights' **Command Structure** sidebar, p.59, names dying **Grand
  Master Gauthier de Dampiere** and his likely successors among the
  eight Commanders of the Tongues, plus **Sir Jehan de la Croix**,
  Commander of the True Cross; the Red Order's **Hagiography** sidebar,
  p.60; the Sisters' **Musical Terminology** glossary, p.66; the
  Murnau's **Family Tree** and **Godparenting** sidebars, pp.69-70).
  Introduces the **Marzonian Rule** (p.74) — the cross-order governance
  code Cardinal Marzone commissioned, modeled on the Red Order's own
  Rule, that settles disputes between orders' individual customs and
  formalizes **the Rule of Three** (a cleric brother and sister may
  never be alone together) and single-sex sleeping/quartering
  requirements; the **Oculi Dei Exemption** sidebar (p.72) notes the
  Eyes are informally exempted from most of it for operational secrecy.
  Covers **cell leadership** (the rector role, election, removal, p.76),
  the **Rank Background** (p.74 sidebar — five-dot scale from likely
  future rector up to secret Sicae Dei membership or Procurator
  Fiscalis), **Chapter-Houses** (public/well-known vs. disguised/
  "hiding in plain sight" vs. secret safe-houses, with a **Pooling
  Backgrounds** sidebar, p.78, on combining players' Chapter-House/
  Flock/Allies dots), **Women in the Inquisition** (papal dispensation
  letting cloistered nuns leave enclosure for field duty, p.78; the lay
  **Beguines** sidebar, p.80, as informal housing for traveling sisters;
  the House of Murnau's and Oculi Dei's very different rules for female
  members), and **Sex and the Single Inquisitor** (pp. 81-83, with a
  **Homosexuality and the Medieval Church** sidebar, p.82) — the gap
  between the Rule's ideal and its actual, forgiveness-weighted
  enforcement, and the unresolved dilemma facing married/family
  Murnau and Oculi Dei inquisitors that the Rule doesn't really
  address.
- **Torture and Punishment** (pp. 83-84): the Inquisition's
  evidence-gathering caution around high-status suspects, why demons are
  destroyed rather than tried, and the **Sicae Dei, "Daggers of God"**
  (pp. 82-83) — a secret internal-affairs order Marzone created after
  discovering an inquisitor, Father Andrea Pitti, was secretly a
  Messianic Voices heretic-sorcerer; works in cross-order cells of
  three, requires unanimous guilt verdicts, and has unquestioned
  authority to arrest and sentence fellow inquisitors.
- **Sample of Play** (pp. 84-89): a full worked transcript (Judith, a
  Sister of St. John; Alberto, a Red Order monk; Thomas, an Oculus Dei)
  investigating child disappearances, culminating in an exorcism of a
  demon-possessed boy — demonstrates Terror rolls, Blessing activation,
  the exorcism *ritus*, and how a cell divides labor between combat,
  faith-based intervention, and support.
**Key themes:** The gap between the Marzonian Rule's ideal (uniform
cross-order discipline) and lived practice (constant informal
exceptions, especially for the Oculi Dei and lay orders); institutional
self-policing (Sicae Dei) as a direct, defensive response to the base
book's Messianic Voices infiltration scare.
**Key terms/entities:** Marzonian Rule, Rule of Three, Sicae Dei,
Rector, Rank (Background), Chapter-House (Background), Gauthier de
Dampiere, Jehan de la Croix, Mother Maria-Immaculada, Father Andrea
Pitti, Judith/Alberto/Thomas (Sample of Play characters).

## Chapter Three: The Ways of the Faithful (pp. 91-127)
**Summary:** Splits into a subjective/social discussion of Conviction,
Blessings, and Curses, followed by dense crunch: a new Holy Art path,
new *ritae*, and full per-order catalogs of new Curses and new
Merits/Flaws.
- **The Lamp of Faith** (pp. 91-94): describes Conviction as lived
  experience and how it's *perceived* by others — the Flock split
  between idolizing and fearing intensely convicted inquisitors; pagans
  reacting to Conviction with proportional hatred regardless of the
  inquisitor's Piety; vampires' contempt curdling to wary respect after
  a first real clash; and **Callousness** specifically as a
  reputation-damaging "public relations" problem for the whole
  Inquisition, not just a personal failing.
- **Blessings** (pp. 94-95) and **Curses** (p.95): surveys in-setting
  *opinion* on the propriety of Blessing use — Orisons seen as benign,
  the Holy Art as legitimate but Red-Order-tainted theurgy, Endowments
  (especially Legacies) viewed with real suspicion even within the
  Church, moderated in practice by Pope Gregory IX's pragmatic
  toleration. Introduces the **Messianic Voices** (Christian mage
  Fellowship) as an external group with its own, sometimes condescending
  opinion of inquisitorial Blessings as "hedge magic." Frames Curses as
  contested in their own theology — most inquisitors read them as holy
  suffering (stigmata) or divine "disincentives," but some (especially
  older Murnau) suspect Curses come from the Adversary, not God.
- **Learning New Blessings, Acquiring New Curses** (pp. 95-96): the
  actual acquisition mechanic — deliberate retreat/prayer to learn
  Blessings safely (banking Conviction up to Superior Virtue + its
  linked basic Virtue); rushing growth through raw Conviction expenditure
  risks a Curse, felt first as dread, then as onset within about a day.
- **Holy Art** (pp. 96-97) and **Holy Ground and Sacred Relics**
  (pp. 97-98): notes the Red Order deliberately gatekeeps teaching the
  Holy Art to other orders (the Monastery of Laurendine, Toulouse, under
  Frère Francois Lerouge, is the only place it's formally taught outside
  the Red Order) and gives Storyteller guidance for playing up holy
  ground/relics as subjectively meaningful, not just mechanically
  useful.
- **Orisons** (pp. 98-101): nine new minor Blessings organized by basic
  Virtue — **Conscience** (Gift of Kindness, Touch of Compassion,
  Aesthetic Eye), **Self-Control** (Loaves and Fishes, Dream of Jacob,
  Carried by Faith), **Courage** (Fear of God, Blessed Vigor, Grace of
  Moses).
- **Endowments** (pp. 101-113): new tiered powers per order —
  **Benedictions** (Oculi Dei): Endurance Everlasting, Divine Light,
  Libera Me, Vow of Silence. **Investitures** (Knights of Acre): Lamb of
  God, Shield of God, Enduring Rock of Faith, Milius Dei. **Legacies**
  (House Murnau): Divine Insight, Surveying the Devil's Flock, Pulling
  the Serpent's Fangs, Fear No Evil. **Psalms** (Sisters of St. John):
  Jubilation, Remembrance Song, Song of the Seraphim, Vox Dei — the last
  ("Dies Irae") destroys buildings sheltering the Enemy outright and
  briefly sanctifies the ground afterward.
- **Holy Art: Corpus Lux** (pp. 113-115): a full new fifth Holy Art path
  ("Body of Light," usable by any order, chosen freely by the Red
  Order), rated Charisma + Corpus Lux — Rebuking the Sight of the
  Tainted (denies a target a reflection), Protection of the Holy Cross,
  Empower the Instrument of Faith (turns any blessed relic into an
  aggravated-damage weapon), Blessed Litany, and Radiant Presence of the
  Lord (a Piety-scaled sunlight-equivalent nimbus).
- **Ritae** (pp. 115-118): eight new formal rituals across five power
  levels — Prayer for Clarity, Blessing of Eternal Rest, Sanctification
  of the Homestead, Finding the Way Through the Word, Veiled Presence of
  the Holy, Tears of the Weeping Heart of Abel (mass-produces holy-water
  vials that do aggravated damage), Donning the Vestments of Sacred
  Resilience, and Investment of the Lasting Sacrifice (forges a
  permanent ward-crucifix).
- **Curses** (pp. 118-124): four new Curses per order-specific category,
  each at Acolyte/Advocate/Judge severity — **Anathemae** (Oculi Dei):
  Contagion, Spy's Bane, Life of Shadows, Sentence of Solitude (this
  book's most severe single Curse — crippling dice penalties from mere
  proximity to other people). **Maledictio** (House Murnau): Immaculate,
  Lux Dei (a literal visible glow), Devil's Rapture, Friend of Fiends.
  **Stigmata** (Red Order): Curse of Salt, Burden of the Cross, Stench
  of Lazarus, Leper's Visage. **Interdictions** (Knights of Acre): True
  Poverty, Cain's Blight, Forsaken, Angel of Wrath. **Visitations**
  (Sisters of St. John): Stray Vision, Possessed Tongue, Slave to
  Visions, A Plague of Angels.
- **Merits and Flaws** (pp. 124-127): new Physical (Tough as Nails, Low
  Pain Threshold), Mental (Talented Liar, David's Aim, Gullible,
  Insomniac, Supernaturally Ignorant), Social (Orthodox, Arrogant,
  Criminal Past, Malicious Zealot, Convert), and Supernatural (Bitter
  Blood, Callous-Sensitive, Grace, Order Prodigy, Saint's Conscience,
  Vampiric Recognition, Divinely Favored, Inviolate, Gifted Exorcist,
  Halo, Devil's Puppet, Curse Prone, Unlucky, Spiritual Cripple) traits.
**Key themes:** Blessings and Curses as socially and theologically
contested within the setting, not neutral game mechanics — every
Endowment category carries an in-world reputational cost that maps to
how "showy" or "sorcerous" it looks to outside observers.
**Key terms/entities:** Corpus Lux, Messianic Voices, Frère Francois
Lerouge, Monastery of Laurendine, Pope Gregory IX, Dies Irae (Vox Dei's
top-tier effect).

## Chapter Four: Soldiers of God, Servants of Hell (pp. 129-143)
**Summary:** Zooms out from individual orders to cell-level and
setting-level material: how cells actually form and evolve, how
non-order inquisitors get recruited, how the Inquisition interacts with
every other supernatural power type in the setting, and six ready-to-use
antagonists.
- **Development of the Inquisitorial Cell** (pp. 130-132): describes
  each order's distinct training-cell pattern (single-gender clerical
  training cells that later merge into mixed-order, mixed-gender field
  cells; von Murnau apprenticed individually to a "godparent" relative;
  Oculi Dei recruited and drilled in isolation from the start) and a
  **The Rise and Fall of an Inquisitor** sidebar (p.131) on three
  failure modes: political climbers who stagnate spiritually, the
  over-driven who burn out into Callousness, and the overly compassionate
  who lose their taste for the hunt. Cells that succeed become "teaching
  cells"; cells that fail or that have an imposed, resented leader can
  be dissolved by the local Council of Faith.
- **Outside the Orders** (pp. 132-136): the Inquisition actively
  recruits secular/monastic clergy and nobility/commoners with no
  connection to the five orders — a **From Beneath the Enemy's Shadow**
  sidebar (p.133) specifically flags heavy recruiting success among
  Benedictines, much less among Franciscans, and almost none among
  Dominicans (whom the shadow Inquisition would dearly like to recruit
  wholesale, given the overlap with the *public* Holy Office's own
  heresy-hunting, but approaches only cautiously). Gives a full
  alternate **character creation process for un-empowered Inquisitors**
  (p.135 sidebar — Conviction capped at 3, no Piety/Callousness, no
  Impulse) and a **Special Cases** discussion (p.135-136) explicitly
  endorsing dual-order player characters (e.g. Red Sister *and* House of
  Murnau) and giving Storytelling guidance for letting Blessing/order
  choices flex to fit an unusual concept rather than forcing concepts to
  fit the printed order benefits.
- **In the Company of Monsters** (pp. 136-138): non-hostile or
  tactically-convenient inquisitor/supernatural contact, broken out by
  splat and (for vampires) by Road — Road of Heaven and Road of Sin
  vampires each destabilize inquisitors in different ways (the former by
  looking genuinely holy, the latter by understanding desire too well),
  Prodigal and Scion vampires are the hardest for an inquisitor to
  excuse regardless of behavior, and Feral vampires are usually mistaken
  for werewolves on sight. Covers Garou culture (mostly theorized, not
  known, by naturalist Theodosians studying the Schwarzwald; tribal
  opinion split between the Christian-leaning Tribes treating
  inquisitors as a useful anti-Wyrm tool and grudge-holding Tribes
  hunting them), mage Traditions/Fellowships (the Messianic Voices ease
  contact; the Valdaermen, Old Faith, and Spirit-Talkers suspect
  Messianic Voice collusion behind a string of bad encounters; the Order
  of Hermes draws attention via its Tremere feud; the Batini remain
  essentially unknown to the Inquisition), and the fae (dismissed by
  most inquisitors as folklore, which is exactly how the Fair Folk
  prefer it — though contact is described as increasing again after
  centuries of mutual disinterest).
- **Antagonists** (pp. 138-143): six ready-made foes. **Byleth** (p.138)
  — a Fallen angel of the sea who slept centuries inside a birch-tree
  seed, now a serial body-thief twice defeated by Red Brother Giordano
  Nicola d'Arzenta and Red Sister Vittoria Santini di Parma, currently
  rebuilding via a cult of French nobility and planning revenge; recently
  allied with Wilhelm von Murnau. **The Pale Brother** (pp.139-140) — a
  vampire whose black monastery and its former captive-turned-source,
  an Oculi Dei testimony smuggled to the Inquisition as an open
  challenge/warning, ties this book directly to the base book's own Pale
  Brother plotline (see `dark-ages-inquisitor` Chapter One). **The
  Circle of Red** (p.140) — a mysterious raiding group whose members wear
  Red Order-style robes but are disavowed by every actual Theodosian
  chapter-house the Poor Knights have checked, currently active near
  Poitiers. **Wilhelm von Murnau** (pp.140-142) — Leopold von Murnau's
  estranged twin brother Weyland's murderer, seduced young by the
  Courts of Winter and Autumn, trained at the black school Scholomance,
  now returned in his prime to destroy his family (starting with
  Leopold) and, on discovering the shadow Inquisition through that
  pursuit, allying with Byleth against it. **Fabrizio Ulfila** (pp.142)
  — a ~1,700-year-old Ventrue merchant of Bologna and former Arian-heresy
  priest embittered at orthodoxy's victory, now covertly funding
  heretical movements (including Cathar refugees) and attempting,
  unsuccessfully so far, to corrupt Dominican inquisitors specifically.
  **Bishop Estaci de Albi** (pp.142-143) — a zealously orthodox
  ex-Cathar-sympathizer's-son bishop near Toulouse whose anti-heresy
  investigations have inadvertently traced real, suspicious patterns
  connecting the Red Order, the Poor Knights, and the House of Murnau;
  the book ends on the open question of whether he'll be recruited,
  silenced, or something worse before he puts the pieces together.
**Key themes:** The shadow Inquisition's secrecy as a double-edged
liability — Bishop Estaci's investigation and the Pale Brother's
taunting testimony both show that operating in the shadows doesn't
guarantee the Inquisition stays hidden; corruption arriving from
*inside* a bloodline (Wilhelm) mirrors the base book's Messianic Voices
infiltration scare at a family scale instead of an institutional one.
**Key terms/entities:** Byleth, the Pale Brother, the Circle of Red,
Wilhelm von Murnau, Weyland von Murnau, Fabrizio Ulfila, Bishop Estaci
de Albi, Scholomance, the Courts of Winter/Autumn.

## Cross-cutting themes

### Direct continuity with `dark-ages-inquisitor`
This book is written to be read *with* the base book open, not as a
standalone. Beyond the shared named leadership (Marzone, Gauthier de
Dampiere, Aignen le Libraire, Rodrigue de Navarre), two threads connect
directly:
- **The Pale Brother.** The base book's Chapter One frame narrative
  (Osoro of Madrid's captivity in the Pale Brother's monastery) and this
  book's Chapter Four antagonist entry (pp.139-140) describe the same
  named vampire and the same "black monastery" — this book adds that the
  Oculi Dei received a smuggled written testimony from inside it as a
  taunting invitation, which the order suppressed rather than acted on.
  Treat the two entries as one throughline, not independent creations.
- **The Sicae Dei's origin story directly answers a structural gap in
  the base book.** `dark-ages-inquisitor` establishes the Messianic
  Voices infiltration scandal (Father Andrea Pitti) as a past event; this
  book (pp.82-83) is where the Inquisition's institutional *response* —
  a permanent internal-affairs order — actually gets built and detailed.

### The Marzonian Rule as an unresolved contradiction, not a solved problem
This book repeatedly sets up its own governance framework only to show
it failing to cover real cases: the Oculi Dei are formally exempted
in practice (p.72) despite no textual exemption; married Murnau and
Oculi Dei inquisitors have no accommodated path under a Rule written by
and for the celibate clerical orders (p.81); and Sex and the Single
Inquisitor (pp.81-83) states outright that enforcement is
confession-and-remorse-weighted rather than uniform. Any question about
"the rules the Inquisition lives by" should flag this gap between stated
Rule and lived practice rather than presenting the Marzonian Rule as
cleanly authoritative.

### Every order's Enemy-taxonomy reveals its institutional psychology
Chapter One's five Enemy-classification systems (see that chapter's
summary above) are the clearest single comparative resource in this
book for questions like "how would order X react to encountering a
vampire/werewolf/warlock" — each order's scheme encodes what it's
actually built to notice and fight, not a neutral monster catalog.

### Named unresolved plot hooks worth flagging to a Storyteller
This book deliberately leaves several threads open rather than
resolving them within its own pages: Fridebraht von Graft's origin
(p.27), the true source of the Notre Dame alchemists' affiliation
(p.32), whether Bishop Estaci de Albi discovers the shadow Inquisition
(p.143), and whether Byleth and Wilhelm von Murnau's new alliance
(p.142) has moved beyond the planning stage. None of these are resolved
elsewhere in this bundle or in `dark-ages-inquisitor`.
