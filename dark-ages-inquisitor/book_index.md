# Dark Ages: Inquisitor — Index

## How to use this file
This is a thematic index. For exact quotes or precise facts, query
book_chunks.db (see README.md in this bundle for query examples) rather
than relying on the summaries below.

## Overview
*Dark Ages: Inquisitor* (White Wolf, 2002) is a sourcebook for the
**original, pre-V20** *Vampire: The Dark Ages* line — the book states
outright that it "is not a complete game" and requires *Dark Ages:
Vampire* for its core rules (Introduction, p.18). It uses the classic
Storyteller System (Attributes/Abilities, Willpower, Virtues rated 1-5,
difficulty-based dice pools), not V20's revised trait set. It supplies a
full toolkit for playing the *hunters* of the setting: the "shadow
Inquisition," a secret organization within the Catholic Church (distinct
from the public Holy Office/Papal Inquisition) formed to fight vampires,
demons, and sorcerers under cover of the historical 13th-century
Inquisition. The book covers the shadow Inquisition's secret history
(interwoven with real 13th-century Church history — the Albigensian
Crusade, the Fourth Lateran Council, real popes), its five constituent
orders, character creation, a full suite of divine powers and their
corrupting price, and Storytelling advice with three sample chronicles.

Framed as secret history, the book treats "vampire" as something most
inquisitors only half-understand — described variously as "the undead,"
"night-demons," "vampyr," or, among those with real knowledge, **Cainites**
following the **Cainite Heresy**. A recurring, book-spanning plot thread
concerns the **Crimson Curia**, a secret Cainite conspiracy embedded
within the Church hierarchy itself — this is the book's central
vampire/Church-crossover hook and its most important connection to the
wider *Dark Ages: Vampire* metaplot (see "The Crimson Curia and the
Cainite Heresy" below).

## Prelude: Final Absolution (pp. 8-17)
**Summary:** A fiction chapter introducing Brother Leopold von Murnau (a
Dominican inquisitor of House Murnau) and Sir Baudioun le Breton (a Poor
Knight of Acre) investigating the massacre of an Inquisition chapter-house
in Foix. The culprit is a demon sent to retrieve a cursed grimoire, the
**Libellus Secretum Sartaelis**, which contains a sentient, manipulative
demonic intelligence named **Sartael** that converses directly with
Leopold in the book's margins — offering knowledge in exchange for trust,
and warning Leopold (accurately, and self-servingly) that the book should
be destroyed. The young monk Brother Renier is revealed to have secretly
bought the book from a wine-seller's Castilian visitor; the ensuing demon
attack kills Renier but is repelled with holy water and a blessed sword.
The chapter closes with a second vignette: **Osoro of Madrid**, an Oculi
Dei "Eye of God," helplessly witnesses a "night-devil" (an unmistakable
vampire, though never named as such) feed on and kill a girl in an alley,
establishing early that inquisitors often encounter Cainites without
being able to name or fully understand them.
**Key themes:** The corrupting lure of forbidden knowledge (Sartael as a
patient tempter, not an obvious monster); the human cost paid by
low-ranking inquisitors; vampires perceived by the Inquisition as
unclassified "demons."
**Key terms/entities:** Leopold von Murnau, Baudioun le Breton, Brother
Renier, Sartael/*Libellus Secretum Sartaelis*, Osoro of Madrid, the Oculi
Dei.

## Introduction (pp. 18-25)
**Summary:** Sets out the game's premise, themes, and structure.
Establishes that *Dark Ages: Inquisitor* requires *Dark Ages: Vampire*
for core rules and is not a *Hunter: The Reckoning* product despite
sharing some themes. Names Faith and Fanaticism as the central themes,
with Desperation as a recurring secondary theme (paralleling the War of
Princes in the core Vampire book). Introduces the mechanic of
**Callousness** — the corruption state an inquisitor falls into when
zeal for the mission outstrips faith in God — as the book's central moral
hazard. Gives a chapter-by-chapter roadmap (which inconsistently calls
Chapter Five "Blessings and Curses" rather than its actual printed title,
"The Word of God" — a minor internal naming slip). Closes with a reading/
viewing list (*The Name of the Rose*, the *Malleus Maleficarum*, the
*Brother Cadfael* novels, etc.) and a lexicon of setting terms (Council
of Faith, Grand Inquisitor, Holy Office, Oculi Dei, the five orders).
**Key themes:** Faith as the load-bearing theme of the whole line;
fanaticism as an occupational hazard, not a character flaw to avoid.
**Key terms/entities:** Callousness, Council of Faith, Grand Inquisitor
Cardinal Battista Marzone, Holy Office (the *public* Papal Inquisition,
explicitly distinct from the *secret* shadow Inquisition).

## Chapter One: Against All Demons (pp. 26-59)
**Summary:** An in-character "bestiary" framed as the written testimony
of **Osoro of Madrid** (from the Prelude), an Oculi Dei "Eye" recounting
his mentor Father Bernard's teachings while the two are held captive in a
monastery run by a vampire "master" (the **Pale Brother**). Interleaves
first-hand horror-story accounts of the Inquisition's enemies with an
ongoing frame narrative: Osoro and Bernard are lured into a monastery,
discover it is a Cainite haven, Osoro kills the traitor monk **Brother
Guy** (revealed to have a demon's eye implanted in his neck) and
impersonates him to spy on the Pale Brother, who reveals himself as a
knowing Cainite operative of the **Crimson Curia** — the chapter's key
reveal (see "Crimson Curia" below). Covers, in-character, several
monster categories: **Willing Servants** (human thralls, illustrated by
a disturbing account of a child-abuse/demon-summoning cult in Paris),
**Warlocks** (mortal sorcerers), **Magical Constructs**, **Demons of
Flesh** (a catch-all that explicitly includes vampires — "a man had to
give his permission to become a blood-drinking horror," unlike other
Demons of Flesh — alongside werewolves and monstrous swamp-creatures),
**Werewolves** (folklore-heavy: silver, fire, packs, breeding with
humans, wolfsbane as a partial remedy), **Grotesques and Monsters**
(minor demons), **The Undead** (a four-stage aging model for vampires:
newly-dead, "truly dead" — the classic vulnerable-to-sun stage —, and a
final decrepit "shambling" stage), **Demons of Ether** (Ghosts, Faeries,
true Demons/fallen angels, **Shadow Demons** believed Moorish in origin,
and rules for demonic **Possession**). Ends with a short vignette of
Leopold von Murnau confronting his corrupted cousin Berthold over the
Sartael grimoire, saved by an unseen ally's crossbow bolt.
**Key themes:** Consent as the dividing line between vampires and other
"Demons of Flesh"; the unreliability and incompleteness of what rank-
and-file inquisitors actually know about their enemies; institutional
infiltration (the Pale Brother's monastery is a functioning Cainite
operation hiding in plain sight within the Church).
**Key terms/entities:** Osoro of Madrid, Father Bernard, Brother Guy, the
Pale Brother, Aignen le Libraire, Rodrigue de Navarre, the Crimson Curia,
Sartael, Berthold, "Demons of Flesh," the four stages of undeath.
**Notable claims/data points:** The undead's final decrepit stage is
described first-hand by Osoro (a leprous, senseless "gargoyle" nobleman
in Tours) — useful color for what "elder" vampires might look like to
mortal witnesses in this line.

## Chapter Two: Call to Arms (pp. 60-119)
**Summary:** The book's largest and most lore-dense chapter: a secret
history of the shadow Inquisition's founding, told mostly through
in-character letters and historical framing woven around **real
13th-century Church history** (the Albigensian Crusade against the
Cathars, the massacre at Béziers, the Fourth Lateran Council of 1215,
real popes Innocent III, Honorius III, and Gregory IX). Cardinal
**Battista Marzone**, appointed Inquisitor-General by Innocent III in
1208, undertakes an incognito "pilgrimage" across Christendom that
convinces him of a hidden supernatural threat, culminating in his
founding, alongside recruits **Gauthier de Dampiere** (a knight who
becomes the shadow Inquisition's founding warrior), **Abbot Gervèse le
Fèvre** and **Sister Teresa of Madrid** (a visionary later Beatified),
of a secret Inquisition parallel to — and largely unknown to — the
public Holy Office founded under Dominic Guzman (later St. Dominic). The
chapter formally establishes the **five orders** (see Chapter Three for
full write-ups): the Poor Knights of the Passion of the Cross of Acre,
the Sisters of St. John, the Order of St. Theodosius ("the Red Order"),
the House of Murnau, and the Oculi Dei ("Eyes of God"). It also details
the Inquisition's internal organization (Councils of Faith, the Supreme
Council, jurisdiction and procedure), the taxonomy of offenses the
Inquisition concerns itself with (Heresy, Schism, Paganism, Islam,
Judaism, Maleficium, Demonolatry), torture procedure, and closes with the
**Cainite Heresy** section — the book's fullest explanation of how
inquisitors understand vampires (see "Crimson Curia and the Cainite
Heresy" below). Includes the martyrdom of Aignen le Libraire (executed by
the *public* Holy Office in 1231 on false heresy charges, explicitly
flagged in a sidebar as an optional metaplot event a troupe can ignore).
**Key themes:** Secret history layered onto real events (the "everything
you know about the 13th-century Church is half-true" conceit); tension
between orthodoxy and operational secrecy; institutional distrust between
orders sharing one cause.
**Key terms/entities:** Cardinal Battista Marzone (Inquisitor-General/
Grand Inquisitor), Gauthier de Dampiere, Abbot Gervèse le Fèvre, Sister
Teresa of Madrid (the "Blessed Teresa"), Aignen le Libraire, Rodrigue de
Navarre, Xalbador (the Cainite whose deception shattered the Sword of St.
James and indirectly created the Oculi Dei), Otto von Murnau, Leopold von
Murnau, the Council of Faith, the Supreme Council, the Cainite Heresy,
Béla — *sic*, i.e. the Albigensian Crusade, the Fourth Lateran Council.
**Notable claims/data points:** The shadow Inquisition is formally
commissioned circa 1215-1219; Sister Teresa dies in 1227 and is Beatified
in 1228; Aignen le Libraire is executed by the Holy Office in 1231 (an
explicitly optional/movable metaplot date).

## Chapter Three: The Mind of the Inquisition (pp. 120-143)
**Summary:** Organizational deep-dive plus full write-ups of the five
orders. Covers the Inquisition's hierarchy (individual inquisitor →
cell → Council of Faith → Supreme Council → Grand Inquisitor Cardinal
Marzone), recruitment by order, titles and promotion, the **Procurator
Fiscalis** (prosecutor/archivist role), trial procedure (capture,
argument, sentencing — "guilty until proven innocent"), checks and
balances between cells and Councils, and the secret, never-exercised
power of the Supreme Council to depose the Grand Inquisitor. Then gives
each of the five orders a full profile (history, appearance, initiation,
organization, holdings, associated Superior Virtue, Blessing/Curse
category, order-specific game benefit and drawback, and each order's
in-character "outlook" on the other four):
- **Poor Knights of the Passion of the Cross of Acre:** military order,
  Grand Master Gauthier de Dampiere, associated with **Investitures**
  and the **Interdictions** Curse category, Superior Virtue **Zeal**.
- **The Red Order (Order of St. Theodosius):** scholarly, coeducational
  order founded by Theodosius (b. 812), specializes in the **Holy Art**
  and is prone to **Stigmata** as its Curse category, Superior Virtue
  **Wisdom**; its "unfortunate matter of Brother Everard de Gascony"
  (a research disaster that destroyed an abbey) still shadows its
  reputation.
- **Sisters of St. John:** visionary order founded by Sister Teresa,
  specializes in **Psalms** and suffers **Visitations** as its Curse
  category, Superior Virtue **Faith**.
- **The House of Murnau:** a Bavarian noble family with a hereditary
  "curse" that lets members smell supernatural corruption; specializes
  in **Legacies** and suffers **Maledictio** as its Curse category,
  Superior Virtue player's choice.
- **Oculi Dei ("Eyes of God"):** lay intelligence network founded by
  Rodrigue de Navarre after the Sword of St. James's disastrous encounter
  with the Cainite Xalbador, commanded in practice by Aignen le Libraire;
  specializes in **Benedictions** and suffers **Anathemae** as its Curse
  category, Superior Virtue **Wisdom**, motto *Credite Nemini* ("Trust no
  one").
**Key themes:** Institutional friction as a design feature, not a bug —
each order's "Outlooks" section is explicitly unflattering about the
other four; the House of Murnau as a rare "lay order" bloodline analog
sitting alongside four clerical/monastic orders.
**Key terms/entities:** Procurator Fiscalis, Council of Faith, Supreme
Council, the five orders' founders and mother-houses (Fortress of the
Cross on Cyprus; Monastery of Laurendine, Toulouse; Convent of St. John
the Divine, Madrid; Castle Murnau, Bavaria; no fixed Oculi Dei
chapter-house by design).

## Chapter Four: Characters and Drama (pp. 144-173)
**Summary:** Character creation rules, explicitly building on *Dark
Ages: Vampire* Chapter Five rather than replacing it. Walks through
concept, Nature/Demeanor/Impulse (Impulses are the "dark extreme" of
each Nature and surface when a character is Callous), Attributes/
Abilities allocation, and the book's new mechanical layer: **Superior
Virtues** (Faith/Wisdom/Zeal, each built on top of a basic Virtue —
Conscience/Self-Control/Courage respectively, extending its cap from 5 to
10), **Conviction** (a 1-10 resource roughly parallel to a vampire's
Willpower-as-fuel, spent to activate Blessings and bank toward new
Superior Virtue dots or Endowments), and **Piety** (a Road-like moral
standing score; an inquisitor becomes **Callous** whenever Conviction
exceeds Piety). Introduces four "power stages" (Servant → Acolyte →
Advocate → Judge, keyed to total Superior Virtue dots) that gate access
to higher-tier Blessings. Adds two new Skills (**Torture**, **Interrogation**)
and four new Backgrounds (**Chapter-House**, **Exposure**, **Flock**,
**Holy Relics**, plus **Rank**), plus a full set of order-flavored Merits
and Flaws and an explicit "Disallowed" list of standard *Vampire* Merits/
Flaws that don't fit inquisitor characters. Closes with rules for
mundane hazards (hunger, thirst, poison, seven period-appropriate
diseases) and torture-of-captives mechanics.
**Key themes:** Explicit intent that inquisitor cells be diverse-order
teams bound by external threat rather than internal harmony; Piety/
Conviction as a moral-hazard economy structurally similar to (but
philosophically opposed to) a vampire's Road/Humanity — the book is
careful to distinguish the two ("A vampire clings to its Road in a
mockery of faith...").
**Key terms/entities:** Superior Virtues (Faith/Wisdom/Zeal), Conviction,
Piety, Callousness, Impulse, the four power stages (Servant/Acolyte/
Advocate/Judge), True Faith (explicitly *not* used as a Trait in this
book — a sidebar gives an approximate conversion instead).

## Chapter Five: The Word of God (pp. 174-217)
**Summary:** The full powers chapter — by far the longest — covering
**Orisons** (minor Blessings available even to ordinary devout mortals,
tied to the three basic Virtues), **Endowments** (order-specific tiered
power sets: **Benedictions** for the Oculi Dei, **Investitures** for the
Knights of Acre, **Legacies** for House Murnau, **Psalms** for the
Sisters of St. John), and **the Holy Art** (the Red Order's specialty,
a three-path theurgy system explicitly modeled on the Trinity: **Sophia
Patris**/"Father's Wisdom" for revelation and truth-detection, **Sedulitas
Filius**/a warrior's path for combat support, **Boni Spiritus**/"Gifts of
the Spirit" for protection and healing — plus **Ritae Solemnis**, formal
multi-step rituals such as the Holy Rite of Exorcism and the **Forge of
God** ritual for crafting blessed weapons). Also covers **Holy Ground**
(Ambient Faith ratings and their mechanical effects on vampires, spirits,
and demons — vampires must resist on Willpower or suffer escalating
damage and eventually Rötschreck) and **Holy Relics** (Artifacts and
Anointings, including named legendary items like the Balm of Gilead and
the Sword of St. James). The chapter's second half is the **Curses**
system: five categories tied to the five orders (**Anathemae**/Oculi Dei,
**Interdictions**/Knights of Acre, **Maledictio**/House Murnau,
**Stigmata**/Red Order, **Visitations**/Sisters of St. John), rules for
acquiring new Curses (chiefly via botched Virtue rolls or over-ambitious
Blessing acquisition), and **Cocytus** — the point of total moral
collapse an inquisitor falls into if accumulated Curse stages exceed
combined Piety and Superior Virtue dots, at which point the character's
Nature *becomes* their Impulse permanently. This is explicitly the
inquisitor-side parallel to a vampire succumbing to the Beast: the same
sourcebook conceit of built-in mechanical corruption used throughout the
Dark Ages line, restated here for the hunters rather than the hunted.
**Key themes:** Power and corruption as inseparable (every route to more
power risks a new Curse); Cocytus as the inquisitor equivalent of losing
one's Humanity/Road entirely — once there, further "spending" only digs
the hole deeper.
**Key terms/entities:** Orisons, Endowments (Benedictions/Investitures/
Legacies/Psalms), the Holy Art (Sophia Patris/Sedulitas Filius/Boni
Spiritus), Ritae Solemnis, Forge of God, Holy Ground/Ambient Faith,
Curses (Anathemae/Interdictions/Maledictio/Stigmata/Visitations),
Cocytus.
**Notable claims/data points:** The full acquisition-of-Curses
probability table and Cocytus formula (combined Curse *stages* vs.
combined Piety + Superior Virtue dots) are worth a direct chunk-database
lookup if a question turns on exact thresholds rather than the general
shape of the system.

## Chapter Six: Storytelling (pp. 218-239)
**Summary:** GM-facing advice on running Dark Medieval horror
responsibly (a content-sensitivity discussion explicitly flagging
religion, sexuality, and violence as topics to check with players about
first), chronicle design (scope, structure — "directed," "many roads,"
and combined approaches — theme and mood selection, with a survey of
period-appropriate themes: Defenders of the Faith, Devil's Choice, Trust
and Betrayal, Faith, Separation), preludes, and pacing advice. Closes
with three fully detailed sample chronicles that double as usable
setting material:
- **"A Greater Love"** — set in a fictional Alpine village, involves a
  named 6th-generation Ventrue (**Ulfila**, now venerated locally as
  "St. Amanda," in torpor) and a 6th-generation Tzimisce voivode
  (**Radovan Istvic**) seeking revenge on his former lover; explicitly
  ties into named historical Cainite Heresy antecedents (Arianism,
  Carpocratianism).
- **"Suffer the Children"** — set in Genoa, involves an infernalist cult
  exploiting a fake "Children's Crusade," references the historical 1212
  Children's Crusade and folds in named V20/classic-line metaplot
  figures and factions: the Ravnos, Setites, and Baali as the real
  culprits behind the historical child-slavery, a 7th-generation
  Lasombra Prince of Genoa (**Roberto d'Agostino**) and the Lasombra
  matriarch **Maria d'Agostino**, and Prince **Saint Regis** of
  Marseilles (cross-referenced to *Dark Ages Europe*).
- **"The Long and Lonely Road"** — a tighter, four-character road-trip
  chronicle escorting a captured Norse rune-sorcerer (**Averki
  Skogarmadur**) through werewolf-haunted forest, cross-referenced to
  *Dark Ages: Mage*.
**Key themes:** Horror as consent-based collaborative fiction; named
antagonists reused from the wider Dark Ages metaplot (Lasombra, Tzimisce,
Ravnos, Setites, Baali) as a deliberate hook tying this book back into
the core *Dark Ages: Vampire* line and its supplements.
**Key terms/entities:** Ulfila/"St. Amanda," Radovan Istvic, Roberto and
Maria d'Agostino, Saint Regis of Marseilles, Rinaldi (the infernalist),
Averki Skogarmadur, the Valdaermen (Norse rune-mage society, cross-
referenced to *Dark Ages: Mage*).

## Cross-cutting themes

### The Crimson Curia and the Cainite Heresy — the book's central vampire/Church crossover
This is the single most important thread for anyone cross-referencing
this book against other titles in the library, and it runs across the
Prelude, Chapter One, and Chapter Two.
- The **Cainite Heresy** is presented as a real, active dualist
  heresy-cult that inquisitors partially understand: vampires ("Cainites")
  are worshiped by mortal followers as spiritual beings descended from
  Cain, offering "salvation" through blood-drinking rituals (Chapter Two,
  "The Cainite Heresy"). Inquisitor opinion is split on what Cainites
  metaphysically *are* — a species of lemure (the risen dead), demons
  merely wearing dead mortals' forms, or something else — and the book
  is careful never to resolve this for the reader, consistent with the
  library's broader pattern of deliberately unreliable in-character
  sourcing (see `library_index.md`'s "Unreliable, plural history" theme).
- The **Crimson Curia** is a secret society of Cainites embedded within
  the Church hierarchy, first named by the Pale Brother in Chapter One
  ("The Curia's agents are everywhere across the land... brought into
  our mysteries by the Blood of Cain and the Door of Will... news...to
  the See of Nod") and referenced again as an ongoing, unresolved threat
  in Chapter Two, where Marzone learns of "vampyr" infiltrating major
  churches from a monk's letter and from Leopold von Murnau's own
  warnings about corruption within the Curia in Rome itself. No book
  material resolves how deep this infiltration goes — it is left as a
  live, book-length mystery hook.
- Named Cainites appear as concrete plot movers rather than abstractions:
  **Xalbador** (whose deception of Rodrigue de Navarre's Sword of St.
  James in 1209 indirectly produced the Oculi Dei — a piece of shadow-
  Inquisition origin story that is *caused* by a vampire), and, in the
  Chapter Six sample chronicles, **Ulfila/"St. Amanda,"** **Radovan
  Istvic**, and the **d'Agostino** Lasombra of Genoa.
- If this bundle is being read alongside a wider library, note that
  *Vampire: The Dark Ages 20th Anniversary Edition* independently
  describes its own, differently-sourced "Cainite Heresy" tied
  specifically to Lasombra clergy — see that library's
  `library_index.md` for the comparison; this book never identifies the
  Lasombra as the Heresy's source itself.

### Vampires described without being named — a book-long narrative device
Consistent with the "unreliable, plural history" pattern already
identified across this library, this book adds a distinct variant:
inquisitors frequently encounter vampires but describe them only through
partial, folk-taxonomic terms — "the undead," "night-demons," "vampyr,"
"blood-drinking lemure." Only inquisitors with direct Cainite Heresy
exposure use "Cainite" with any confidence. This is most vivid in the
Prelude (Osoro watches a vampire feed and calls it a "night-devil"
without further understanding) and in Chapter One's "Demons of Flesh"
taxonomy, which folds vampires in as one sub-category among several
without singling them out — readers who already know *Dark Ages:
Vampire* will recognize the creature description in the "final stage of
undeath" as a match for what other library books call very old, wasted
Cainites, but the inquisitor narrator never makes that connection
explicit.

### Mirrored corruption mechanics: Callousness/Cocytus as the hunter's Beast
The book explicitly and repeatedly draws — and then complicates — the
parallel between vampiric corruption (the Beast, Road/Humanity loss) and
inquisitorial corruption (Callousness, Piety, Cocytus). Chapter Four's
Piety section states outright that the *mechanics* resemble a vampire's
Road rating but the *intent* is opposite: "A vampire clings to its Road
in a mockery of faith, trying only to stave off the fury that is the
Beast. The very measure of an inquisitor's spiritual worth is her Piety."
Cocytus (total moral collapse, Chapter Five) is this book's answer to
final vampiric frenzy or "the Beast winning" — the inquisitor's Nature
permanently becomes their Impulse. This gives the library a genuine
structural rhyme worth citing if a cross-book question asks how
"corruption" is modeled for hunters versus their prey across the
collection.

### Real history as secret history
Unlike the library's other classic-line books, which build wholly
fictional Dark Medieval chronology, this book interleaves its shadow-
Inquisition founding narrative directly with attested real history: the
Albigensian Crusade and the massacre at Béziers (1209), the Fourth
Lateran Council (1215), and the reigns of real popes Innocent III,
Honorius III, and Gregory IX are all used as the literal scaffolding on
which Cardinal Marzone's secret order is built. This is a different
technique from `v20-dark-ages`'s in-fiction "Apocrypha of the Clans"
letters or `clanbook-salubri`'s frame-narrative scrivener device — here
the unreliability is not in the narrators disagreeing with each other,
but in the reader being invited to believe a *secret* layer sits behind
*documented* events.
