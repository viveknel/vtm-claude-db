## Overview
A modern-nights (Revised Edition, c.2000) sourcebook for Clan Toreador,
the "Degenerates" or "Artisans" — aesthetes obsessed with beauty, art,
and human culture. Chapter One is framed as a dialogue between a young
Toreador (Carmelita) and an ancient elder (Katherine) recounting a
deliberately *non-standard* clan origin myth that contradicts common
Kindred assumptions (their founder was not Arikel the sculptor, and the
clan was not always associated with roses). Covers ancient/classical
history, the clan's relationship to mortal culture and artists, internal
guild politics, relations with other clans, and character material.

## Chapter One: The Civilized Ones (pp. 13-45)
**Summary:** A frame-narrative interview between Carmelita and the elder
Katherine ("K"), corroborated with occasional in-text editorial notes.
- **Origin myth:** Caine's first Embrace produced **two "perfect
  lovers,"** so devoted they despaired at being unable to have children
  and walked into the sun; grief-stricken, Caine swore never to Embrace
  again but eventually created **Zillah, Irad, and Enosch**, who in turn
  Embraced the 13 founders of the clans. After the "12" killed "the
  three" (the patricide event referenced across the line) and Caine
  cursed 12 of the 13, only **Ishtar** (a bull-dancer, not a sculptor)
  went uncursed, having refrained from the killing — she is revealed as
  the *true* Toreador founder, and "Toreador" was originally a
  description ("bull-leaper"), not a name; the clan was originally
  "Clan of the Blossom" (likely a lily, not a rose). Ishtar fled to Sumer
  (worshipped there, Embracing her lover Tammuz), was pursued by the
  Gangrel Antediluvian **Ennoia** (posing as "Enkidu") over the disputed
  **Tablet of Destiny**, lost Tammuz to a rescue that cost him the Final
  Death, then moved on to Crete, where she taught bull-leaping and
  eventually Embraced King Minos's son at his request.
- **Mycenae:** An early cautionary tale — too many Toreador vying for
  direct rule of Mycenae destroyed each other and left the nation
  vulnerable to Dorian invasion, teaching the clan that lesser childer
  (unlike Caine) cannot successfully rule openly.
- **Rome, Carthage, and the Punic Wars:** Toreador's alliance with the
  Ventrue (Toreador supplied beauty/culture, Ventrue supplied practical
  governance) versus Brujah-ruled Carthage, contextualizing the Punic
  Wars as an early proto-Jyhad between clan-aligned cities.
- **Byzantium, the Vikings, the Crusades, the Inquisition:** Toreador
  influence preserving classical culture in the East; conversion
  narratives; extensive Inquisition-era persecution (including in-
  character description of Toreador tortured/executed).
- **Africa, Asia, Spain, colonial America:** Toreador involvement in
  African cultural sites (e.g. Sungbo's Eredo), limited/uneasy access to
  Deshima-era Japan, the Spanish Inquisition and Goya, and a critical
  take on Columbus/colonization (juxtaposing the heroic U.S. Capitol
  painting against a contemporary alternate woodcut).
- **Modern era:** 20th-century shifts in art (mass media, professional
  art training, "found object" art, electronic culture), ending on the
  clan's ambivalent relationship to a fractured, hyper-mediated new
  millennium.
**Key themes:** Deliberate contradiction of received/popular clan lore
(Arikel, roses) as a structural statement about Toreador's own myth-
making; art and beauty as historically load-bearing (clan involvement
shapes real cultural/political events across every era); a recurring
warning against direct political rule (Mycenae) in favor of influence.
**Key terms/entities:** Ishtar (Toreador founder), Zillah/Irad/Enosch,
Ennoia/"Enkidu," the Tablet of Destiny, Tammuz, Minos.

## Chapter Two: Aesthete Unveiled (pp. 46-77)
**Summary:** Covers the clan's philosophy and social structure.
- **Being Human / relationship to mortals:** Toreador's central paradox
  — deep entanglement with and dependence on human culture and artists,
  including hard questions about whether to Embrace a dying genius; "The
  Sins of Humanity" catalogs predatory/obsessive Toreador behavior
  (stalking, abusive "collecting" of mortal proteges).
- **Clan Hierarchy, Guilds, and Carnivale:** Internal Toreador social
  organization by artistic discipline/guild, with codes of conduct and
  invitation-only balls; prestige and status tied to artistic
  achievement and taste rather than raw power.
- **Elysium and politics:** Toreador's genuine strength in Camarilla
  politics — often underestimated by other clans due to stereotypes of
  frivolity, which the clan exploits; archon service, funding start-ups
  and cultural institutions as long-game influence tools.
- **Relations with other clans:** Notably wary/respectful treatment of
  Malkavians ("the most dangerous clan in the Camarilla"), pragmatic
  distrust of Assamites, disgust at Setite predation philosophy, and
  ambivalence toward mages and other supernatural factions.
- **Game material:** New Discipline powers including **Crocodile's
  Tongue** (Auspex 6, detecting insincerity/dense speech) and art-magic
  powers (paintings capturing a subject's true Nature), plus Merits and
  Flaws.
**Key themes:** The clan's core tension between genuine aesthetic passion
and predatory obsession; political competence disguised by a "flighty"
reputation.

## Chapter Three: The Registry (pp. 78-101)
**Summary:** Character templates: **The Folk Artist**, **The Reclusive
Author**, **Thespian Spy**, **Embraced Restaurateur**, **Liaison to the
Anarchs**, **Pack Priest** (a Sabbat Toreador who won his position by
force), **Graffiti Vandal**, **Queen of the Damned**, **Head**. Closes
with a "Toreador of Note" section including a full backstory for
**Katherine** (the elder interviewed in Chapter One) — revealing she
survived an abusive mortal marriage before her Embrace, later humiliated
her abuser publicly at Elysium — plus other named elders and a
freelance "artifact recovery" specialist.
**Key themes:** Range from sympathetic craftsmen to genuinely monstrous
figures (Pack Priest, Queen of the Damned), and the closing Katherine
profile retroactively recontextualizes Chapter One's frame-narrator as a
survivor whose obsession with beauty and control has personal roots.

## Cross-cutting themes
- **Revisionist self-mythology**: the book repeatedly and deliberately
  contradicts "common" Toreador lore other sourcebooks might assume
  (founder identity, rose iconography), framing even in-universe
  "common knowledge" as unreliable.
- **Beauty/art as genuine historical force**: Toreador influence is
  presented as materially shaping real-world cultural and political
  history across every era covered, not merely decorative.
- **The predator beneath the aesthete**: Chapter Two's "Sins of
  Humanity" and several Chapter Three templates (Pack Priest, Queen of
  the Damned) puncture the clan's elegant reputation with genuine
  monstrousness.
- **Influence over rule**: the Mycenae cautionary tale in Ch.1 and the
  political-influence strategies in Ch.2 both reinforce that Toreador
  power works through cultural/social leverage, not direct control.
